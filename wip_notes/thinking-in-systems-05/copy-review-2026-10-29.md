## Overall assessment

The argument is strongest when it describes a person asked to take responsibility
for a judgment they cannot reasonably make. The thirty-second review, the mismatch
between customer knowledge and technical expertise, and the question of how a new
employee learns the work are the essay's most persuasive material.

The main voice problem is over-explanation. Nearly every section introduces an
idea, explains it, announces its significance, qualifies it, and restates it as a
general principle. The reader keeps being prepared for a point they've already
understood. Giving many of these sentences their own paragraphs makes the effect
more pronounced: the essay sounds rehearsed even when individual sentences are
perfectly natural.

The remedy is a clearer progression and more visible judgment from the writer.
Contractions and jokes alone would leave the underlying cadence intact. The edit
keeps the research, uses the fictional proposal workflow more consistently, and
lets connected paragraphs carry an explanation before pausing for emphasis.

## Priority findings and draft decisions

### 1. Stop announcing that a distinction matters

The source repeatedly uses “The distinction is important,” “This is an important
distinction,” “Consider the implications,” and “Nevertheless, they challenge a
remarkably persistent assumption.” These sentences instruct the reader to notice
something without giving them anything new to notice.

**Original, after the meta-analysis finding:**

> This is an important distinction.
>
> Adding AI can improve human performance without producing a system that is
> better than its best component.

**Draft replacement:**

> That leaves us with two different claims: AI can help a person perform better,
> while the combined system can still fall short of its best component.

The replacement states the distinction directly. The surrounding paragraph then
moves to task differences. The research's comparison and qualifications remain.

### 2. Let the opening stay with the person

The opening button and impossible review time are effective. It then moves through
several broad announcements about AI adoption, a “deeper problem,” two assumptions,
and the need to reconsider where thinking happens. That postpones the next idea.

**Original transition:**

> The arrangement sounds reasonable. Machines handle the repetitive work. Humans
> provide the judgment.
>
> Unfortunately, putting those two things together doesn't guarantee that either
> will happen.

**Draft replacement:**

> You can see the appeal of this arrangement. Let AI do the routine work, then have
> a person catch anything it gets wrong. But catching a mistake is work too.
> Someone needs to know what to look for, have time to look, and be able to do
> something about it.

This keeps the reader near the actual assignment. It also avoids the source's
slightly loose suggestion that adding review could prevent the routine work from
happening: the argument here concerns the adequacy of judgment.

### 3. Give the history a reason to enter the conversation

Hutchins, Parasuraman, Bainbridge, Vaccaro, and Lee enter through similar
author/year/research-description openings. The historical connections are useful,
but this repeated order makes the essay feel like a literature review.

The edit opens the navigation section on the bridge and introduces Hutchins when
the reader sees the problem with studying each crew member separately. It opens
the supervision section with the proposal reviewer, then brings in Bainbridge.
The expertise section starts with the new employee's predicament before presenting
the survey. The studies still have names, dates, and links.

### 4. Give short paragraphs a job

“That can be enormously useful,” “Those are also decisions,” “These are separate
questions,” and “That would be a meaningful improvement” are all isolated in the
source. Most are better absorbed into an explanation or removed.

The draft keeps a few deliberate pauses: the Reject button, the upstream-decision
sentence, and the final question. Elsewhere, paragraphs develop one thought over
several sentences. This restores variation without imposing a fixed paragraph
length or eliminating the author's em dashes.

### 5. Make the workplace observations specific

**Original, after the description of superficial review:**

> We've created a system that depends on human judgment while simultaneously
> discouraging the conditions required to exercise it.

**Draft replacement in context:**

> If the business case for automation assumes people will spend almost no time
> reviewing the results, careful review becomes an inconvenience to the plan.
> We budget thirty seconds, then ask why someone didn't do twenty minutes of
> checking.

This is the kind of dry observation that suits the earlier essays. It names the
organizational contradiction without blaming the reviewer or inventing a personal
incident. The next paragraph asks what the reviewer can actually inspect and do.

One lighter aside follows the shared-calendar example: “Hopefully.” It is optional
voice, not necessary explanation. Other personable phrasing includes “I'd start
with five questions” and “Before celebrating the percentage, I'd want to read the
other four.” These express a point of view without claiming invented work history.

### 6. Separate the sections' contributions

The source revisits the insufficiency of approval in the opening, delegation,
supervision, proposal, oversight, and conclusion sections. Some return is useful,
but each return should add something.

The draft retains the nine-section argument with these distinct jobs:

1. Distributed cognition: why the person alone is an incomplete unit of analysis.
2. Delegation: which decisions have already happened before approval.
3. Supervision: what the reviewer must do under actual working conditions.
4. Collaboration: whether adding a person improves the measured outcome.
5. Expertise: how people learn and maintain the judgment the workflow needs.
6. Proposal design: which decisions can be defined in advance and which need review.
7. Oversight: five questions that make a particular human role assessable.
8. Evaluation: how to test that role, including mistaken acceptance and rejection.
9. Responsibility: who must govern the arrangement, then the button callback.

The Bainbridge section stays focused on the immediate supervision problem. The
later section develops the training problem instead of re-explaining vigilance.
The evaluation section is shorter because the preceding essay already develops
system-level reliability; its new emphasis is the reviewer's performance.

### 7. Make the practical example carry its assumptions

These are substantive clarifications, beyond a voice edit:

- The company and its assistant are explicitly fictional. Later scenarios remain
  hypotheses, with “suppose” marking additions such as timed automatic sending.
- The routine proposal's authorization covers content, transformations, customer
  scope, and price. A maintained source alone cannot authorize a new use.
- Deciding which case is routine is itself a possible point of failure. The edit
  calls for testing that routing and holding uncertain cases.
- The five review questions remain the essay's heuristic, explicitly separate
  from NIST's guidance and any claim of scientific validation.
- Approval may serve authority over a commitment as well as accuracy. A performance
  comparison alone does not determine who may make that commitment.
- A system should be tested on valid recommendations as well as errors: a reviewer
  can harm the outcome by overriding a correct result.

### 8. Keep the qualifications that change the claim

The draft does not generalize Bainbridge's industrial observations into a measured
AI failure rate, the 2025 self-reports into proof of long-term skill decline, or
the content-creation result into established synergy. Those qualifications are
part of the explanation, not symptoms of an impersonal voice.

The source checks produced small precision changes: the meta-analysis's publication
window is January 2020–June 2023, not “predominantly” that period; the 2021 interface
experiment used simulated AI in a food-substitution task. Two primary references
now support the previously uncited automation-bias and algorithm-aversion claims.
See the [brief](brief.md) for sources and verification limits.

### 9. Let the conclusion end once

The original final section has several possible endings: distributed ambiguity,
humans' fallibility, a lesson about intelligence, the division of cognitive work,
three consecutive “We can” statements, responsibility, and the button.

The draft keeps responsibility as a separate organizational decision, returns to
the actual reviewer, and ends with:

> We can distribute the work of thinking. We still have to assign responsibility
> for how the work is done.
>
> Which brings us back to the button labeled Approve.
>
> **What has to be true for that click to mean anything?**

This preserves the original ending's question without the repeated phrase “The
interesting question” or another recap of every claim in the essay.

## What to preserve during further review

- The title, subtitle, opening button, and thirty-seconds/twenty-minutes mismatch.
- The distinction between authority and the practical means to exercise it.
- Distributed cognition as an explanatory perspective, without inferring consciousness.
- The possibility that automation can improve outcomes without individual review
  of every result; more human participation is not assumed to be better.
- The new employee's route to expertise, which deserves its own section.
- The separation between source permission, factual judgment, and authority to
  make a customer commitment.
- Research limits and the heuristic's status as a practical synthesis.

The draft retains all ten original works and adds two supporting references.
