The Thermometer Is in the Wrong Room

Why reliable AI systems depend on more than accurate outputs, successful tests, and increasingly sophisticated models.

By Chris Yasuda Drysdale · Reading time calculated on load

Begin reading ↓

In this essay

The dashboard was green.

A marketing team had recently introduced an AI-powered production system. It took approved briefs, retrieved verified product information, generated copy, and placed that copy into standardized templates.

The results looked promising. In its first month, the team produced 200 assets. Every draft received editorial approval, and an additional quality assurance sample of 40 drafts achieved a 95% pass rate. Leadership was pleased. The team was already discussing how to increase production eightfold without hiring eight times as many people.

Then two published assets needed corrections.

Both contained product claims that were missing important qualifications. The team had approved the correct language, yet the published materials said something different. Attention turned to the AI.

Perhaps its instructions weren't sufficiently precise. Perhaps the model needed better examples. Maybe the team should introduce a second AI to review the first one's work.

All reasonable suggestions, assuming the AI was responsible.

But the AI had generated the correct copy. Editorial had approved it. The errors appeared afterward, when an automated layout process shortened text to fit the available space. The shortening process had done exactly what it was designed to do.

Making text fit and preserving the meaning of a product claim are not necessarily the same thing. Nothing checked whether the approved language had changed before publication.

The system's quality dashboard still reported 95%. Nothing on it reflected the two corrections.

This is a fictional scenario. The workflow introduced errors after its final quality check, and nothing caught them before publication. The dashboard was accurately measuring one part of the process. It simply wasn't measuring the outcome that mattered.

The thermometer was in the wrong room.

How do we design reliable systems when we can't fully predict how they'll behave?

01 Feedback and reliability

Predictable and reliable are not the same thing

Consider two machines intended to maintain a room at a comfortable temperature. The first produces a fixed amount of heat, carefully calibrated to keep the room at 70°F under the expected conditions. The second measures the room's temperature and adjusts its heat output accordingly.

Now someone opens a window.

The first machine continues operating exactly as designed, even as the room becomes colder. The second detects the change and increases its output. Assuming it has enough heating capacity and responds in time, it can bring the temperature back toward the target.

Which machine would you trust to keep the room comfortable? Probably the second. Its output varies because the conditions vary. Its rules can be fixed, but the timing of its response depends on what happens in the room: we can't say when it will switch on without knowing when the window opens and how quickly the room cools.

The first machine, meanwhile, can be perfectly consistent and consistently fail to keep anyone warm.

Predictability concerns what a system will do. Reliability concerns whether it continues to do what is required under the conditions in which it operates.

The distinction depends on what we expect from the system. If we're designing a machine to produce an identical amount of heat every time, the first machine might be perfectly reliable. If we're designing one to maintain a comfortable room, it isn't. Reliability is always relative to a requirement.

A language model might produce slightly different answers to equivalent questions. Those differences could result from its generation process, variations in context, or changes in the surrounding workflow. But requiring identical wording is not necessarily the same as requiring reliable behavior.

An assistant that uses different language to communicate the same accurate information may be working perfectly well. One that consistently produces the same incorrect information is highly predictable and not particularly useful.

Variability alone doesn't tell us whether a system is dependable. What matters is whether its behavior remains within acceptable limits, and what happens when it doesn't.

What feedback changes

A mechanical device regulating its temperature, a human body maintaining its internal conditions, and an automated machine correcting its movements have something in common. Each uses information about what is happening to guide its response.

That connection helped shape cybernetics, the study of communication, control, and regulation across mechanical, biological, and social systems. Mathematician Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine, first published in 1948, helped establish the field.

A central concept was feedback: information about a system's behavior or effects influences what the system does next.

Our thermostat has a desired temperature, a sensor that measures the actual temperature, and a mechanism that changes the heating system's behavior. If the room becomes too cold, it activates the heat. As the temperature rises, it eventually turns it off. It doesn't need to predict every open door; it needs to detect a meaningful deviation and respond in time.

But it responds to the temperature where its sensor is. Put that sensor in a warm hallway, and the thermostat can switch the heater off while the room we care about is still cold.

The thermostat uses negative feedback: when the measured temperature falls below its target, the heater counteracts that change. The word negative doesn't mean harmful. It describes the direction of the feedback's effect.

Positive feedback reinforces a change. Imagine a recommendation system that promotes already-popular content. Increased visibility attracts more engagement, which causes the content to be promoted even further. Neither form is inherently good or bad. The question is what behavior the feedback encourages and whether that serves the system's purpose.

And feedback is not automatically stabilizing. A heating system that measures temperature too infrequently might overheat the room before detecting the change. It then switches off, allowing the temperature to fall too far before responding again. The result can be oscillation: repeated overshooting and overcorrection.

Imagine one model generates a document, a second critiques it, and the first revises its answer. The critic identifies another potential problem. The generator revises again. Eventually, the document has been rewritten six times.

Is it six revisions better? It does at least have a higher version number.

Without meaningful standards for evaluating the revisions and a sensible stopping condition, the models might simply alternate between competing interpretations. Each correction may introduce a new error. Useful feedback tells us whether the work is improving and arrives in time to guide a response.

A system must be able to handle the problems it encounters

A thermostat can measure the room's temperature perfectly and still be connected to a broken heater. Knowing the room is cold doesn't give it a way to warm it.

A second limit is the range of available responses. In An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956), British psychiatrist and cybernetician W. Ross Ashby developed the Law of Requisite Variety. His formal treatment relates disturbances, regulatory responses, and the range of possible outcomes. The practical lesson is that regulation depends on having responses suited to the disturbances that matter.

Consider an AI assistant used to answer employees' questions. Suppose its only available behavior is to generate an answer. A more sophisticated model won't give the surrounding system a way to defer a question or escalate a decision.

Giving the assistant more freedom might help it handle unfamiliar situations, but it also creates opportunities for new mistakes. Sometimes the better solution is to increase its capacity to respond. Sometimes it's to reduce the variety of situations it's expected to handle.

An assistant restricted to a well-defined set of tasks, supported by authoritative information and clear escalation paths, may be substantially more dependable than one encouraged to handle everything.

What Ashby's law does—and doesn't—give us

Chapter 11 develops a mathematical limit under specific assumptions about disturbances and responses. The assistant example is an engineering interpretation, not a formula for choosing a model or counting its tools. More available actions do not by themselves establish effective regulation.

A system cannot reliably manage conditions it is unable to distinguish or respond to effectively. That includes conditions in which the appropriate response is to stop.

02 Seeing the failure

You cannot correct what you cannot see

Let's return to our fictional marketing team. The quality dashboard reports a 95% pass rate. What, exactly, does that number tell us?

Forty drafts received additional quality checks. Thirty-eight passed. The 95% describes those reviewed drafts before layout.

The two published errors tell us the workflow failed after review. With no systematic check of final assets, we don't know how many more errors went unnoticed.

At 200 assets, a sample of 40 represents 20% of production. At 1,600 assets, it represents 2.5%. That change alone doesn't tell us how informative the sample is: sample size, variation in the work, and the precision we need all matter. But even reviewing every draft would leave this particular gap untouched. The workflow can still introduce errors afterward.

This is an observability problem. In software operations, observability concerns our ability to understand what a system is doing through evidence such as logs, metrics, and traces of its execution. The OpenTelemetry primer provides a useful introduction.

A term with two related meanings

In formal control theory, observability concerns whether a system's internal state can be reconstructed from measurements over time, given its dynamics and known inputs. Rudolf Kálmán's work around 1960 helped establish the concept. Software engineering uses the term more broadly; keeping useful logs does not establish observability in the mathematical sense. TU Wien's lecture notes (PDF) introduce the formal definition.

For our workflow, the practical question is: When something goes wrong, can we determine what happened and where?

An incorrect claim might originate in an obsolete reference document, the retrieval of the wrong source, or a model's misinterpretation of accurate information. Or the model might produce a correct answer that another component changes. If all we retain is the final document, we may struggle to tell which happened.

The team can investigate where a claim changed by comparing source versions, retrieved information, the generated draft, editorial changes, layout transformations, and the published output. Missing records, ambiguous language, or failures in other systems can still prevent a complete reconstruction.

The editor approved accurate copy. The layout automation shortened it to fit. The publishing process distributed the resulting asset. Each component accomplished its immediate task, but no step preserved the accuracy of the claim throughout the process.

The failure lived between the steps.

Essay one traces an answer from its source through verification. Essay two asks where to draw the system boundary. Essay three examines the information within that boundary: its source, organization, retrieval, and application. Here, we carry the investigation past the approved answer into transformation and publication.

A dashboard is not a safety mechanism

Imagine a dashboard that reports how many incorrect claims were published last week. It may reveal trends or justify an investigation. By itself, though, it does nothing to prevent the next incorrect claim from being published.

This is the difference between monitoring and regulation. Monitoring makes behavior visible. Regulation uses information about that behavior to influence what happens next.

In March 2026, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology published Challenges to the Monitoring of Deployed AI Systems. Drawing on practitioner workshops and a literature review, it examines monitoring after deployment, where changing inputs, environments, and interactions can expose behavior that earlier evaluations missed.

The report also describes a field still developing its methods. Standardized, validated approaches are not available for every monitoring problem. Some failures are difficult to define, much less detect automatically, and monitoring itself can introduce privacy, security, and resource costs.

A corrective process needs a way to act on what monitoring reveals: reject an output, revise it, retry a failed operation, request clarification, route an exception to a specialist, or prevent a consequential action. In our marketing workflow, detecting changed copy is only part of the solution. Someone—or something—must determine whether the change is acceptable, with authority to stop publication when it isn't.

Otherwise, we've simply built a more sophisticated way to observe failure.

Does another AI solve the problem?

A second model might identify unsupported claims, inconsistent terminology, or missing context that the generating model overlooked. But the reviewer has limitations of its own.

A 2025 study by Shi and colleagues examined position bias in language-model evaluations: judgments influenced by the order in which candidate responses appear. The researchers compared judgments of pairs and lists of responses on two benchmarks, MTBench and DevBench. The effects varied across judges and tasks; they don't establish an error rate for reviewing our marketing assets.

The reviewer has to be tested like any other component. A separate review step doesn't automatically provide independent evidence of correctness. Two models may overlook the same ambiguity or rely on similarly inadequate information. A reviewer may also reject a correct answer or approve a plausible but incorrect one.

The useful questions are whether the reviewer detects the relevant failures, how often it misses them or raises false alarms, and what happens when its judgment is uncertain.

For our missing qualifications, one option is to ask a second AI to inspect every finished asset. A more direct first step is to preserve the approved text and compare it with the text in the final layout. Software can detect a changed passage without deciding what the change means.

Not every change is unsafe. A shorter sentence might preserve its meaning perfectly well; a minor alteration might change the scope of a claim. Deciding whether a change preserves the claim may require a language model, a qualified reviewer, or a carefully defined combination of both.

The finished layout needs checking too. A qualification can remain in a document's underlying text while being clipped, hidden, or unreadable on the page.

And if space is routinely tight, the team can prepare for that before layout begins. It can approve short variants in advance, protect critical qualifications from automatic removal, or use a template that accommodates the required language.

The best safeguard is not necessarily the most sophisticated one. It's the one that addresses the relevant failure mechanism with acceptable cost and risk.

Every additional model, approval stage, and transformation creates another dependency. An extra check can provide useful protection, but its decisions take time and its failures need handling. A workflow that repeatedly sends a document between competing evaluators may be harder to operate than one with a well-placed validation step.

If the model is producing inaccurate drafts, improving its instructions or supporting context may help. If a downstream process is corrupting accurate information, those changes won't repair it. And if no adequate safeguards exist for the intended task, restricting the scope may be the better intervention.

03 Requirements and responsibility

How reliable is reliable enough?

Suppose an AI assistant answers 99% of questions correctly. Is that good?

For an optional brainstorming tool, it might be excellent. For a system generating legally consequential statements, a 1% error rate could be unacceptable, particularly if errors reach the public without verification. At 10,000 comparable operations, a true 1% failure rate corresponds to roughly 100 failures on average.

And what are those failures? An awkward headline and an inaccurate legal qualification shouldn't count as equivalent problems just because each occupies one row in a spreadsheet.

Severity, operating conditions, and the ability to detect and contain failures all matter. A system with occasional harmless variations but effective controls on dangerous outputs may be more dependable for its purpose than one with a higher average accuracy score and no safeguards.

Google's Site Reliability Engineering approach recognizes that pursuing higher reliability involves costs. Engineering resources spent preventing increasingly rare failures are unavailable for other improvements. Its service-level indicators and objectives distinguish what is measured from the performance target the service is expected to meet.

Reliability targets need to reflect what users need, with a clear response when those targets aren't met. That doesn't mean accepting a fixed percentage of every kind of mistake.

Some failures may be unacceptable regardless of how rarely they occur. An average accuracy score cannot demonstrate that those failures are prevented. The workflow may need stronger preventive controls, independent validation, restricted authority, or human approval. If it cannot demonstrate adequate reliability, the appropriate response may be to delay deployment or narrow the task.

Reliability has to survive contact with reality

A successful evaluation tells us how a system performed under the conditions tested. It doesn't guarantee the same performance when those conditions change. Different checks serve different purposes:

01 Before deployment
Test representative tasks, meaningful edge cases, and known failures. For the marketing team, include claims with qualifications and templates with tight space limits.
02 During execution
Protect consequential actions. Hold an asset when approved claim text changes, and verify the correction before publication.
03 After deployment
Look for failures and new conditions in practice. A rise in exceptions after a template update should trigger investigation; a missed error needs a correction and recovery path.

Together, these checks help the team find errors, limit their effects, and improve the workflow. Some failures still won't be visible until a customer reports them.

Standards of correctness can also be subjective or contested, and some risks can't be measured reliably with the evidence available. If the organization cannot tell whether a critical requirement is being met, that uncertainty should influence what the system is allowed to do.

Closing the loop

A dependable workflow connects its requirements to what happens in practice: Define → Observe → Compare → Respond. Then observe again to determine whether the response worked. This is a practical synthesis of control and reliability principles, not a formal theorem.

For a workflow of your own, ask: What outcome are we trying to preserve? What evidence would show it is failing? How do we distinguish acceptable variation from consequential error? What actions are available, and who has the authority to take them?

A limited pilot would give the team evidence to consider before scaling eightfold. It could use representative assets, with missing qualifications planted on purpose. Do the controls catch them? Does an independent check of the final layouts agree? If missed failures, false alarms, and review delays stay within agreed limits, the team can raise volume in stages, rechecking those limits at each step.

Review delays matter here. A check that sends every asset to an already-overloaded reviewer has created a queue. The team still needs someone who can resolve it.

There is also a distinction between correcting an output and learning in the machine-learning sense. Restoring approved copy is an operational correction. Redesigning layout after repeated incidents is a process improvement. Retraining the model with new data is model learning.

All three can affect reliability. A workflow can become substantially more dependable without modifying the model at all.

Someone still has to decide what matters

A thermostat knows whether the room is too cold because someone selected a desired temperature. It can measure the difference and activate the heater. It doesn't independently determine what temperature the occupants ought to prefer.

An AI workflow can likewise measure performance against a defined objective and sometimes correct deviations automatically. But someone still has to decide which outcomes matter.

Our layout automation prioritized fitting text into the available space. That was a reasonable objective, but it was insufficient. The organization also needed to preserve the accuracy of product claims, including qualifications that made the copy less convenient to place.

Should the system shorten the copy, change the template, delay production, or request an exception? Production efficiency alone cannot settle that question. These are organizational decisions involving risk, authority, and accountability.

A functioning feedback loop can consistently deliver an outcome that people don't want if its objective is poorly chosen. More capable automation makes the choice of objective more consequential. It doesn't relieve anyone of responsibility for it.

Our fictional marketing team began with a green dashboard and a reasonable belief that its workflow was performing well. The dashboard wasn't lying. It was reporting what it had been built to measure. The mistake was treating that measurement as evidence for the reliability of the entire system.

Before trusting an automated workflow, we need a defensible answer to a practical question:

When this system gets something wrong, how will it know—and what happens next?

If we can't answer that question, another green dashboard probably won't help. We might just need to move the thermometer.
