When AI Gets the
Right Answer for
the Wrong Reasons

What 2,400 years of philosophy can teach us about knowledge, understanding, and building trustworthy AI systems.

By Chris Yasuda Drysdale · About 20 min read

Begin reading ↓

In this essay

An inquiry into what makes an answer worth trusting.

Imagine two students taking the same exam.

The first has spent months studying. They understand the subject, can explain the underlying principles, and know how to apply what they’ve learned to unfamiliar problems. The second has memorized the answer key.

Both receive perfect scores. Did they demonstrate the same knowledge?

Most of us would instinctively say no. The first student understands the material. The second merely knows which answers to provide.

But let’s complicate things. Suppose the second student can also explain every answer convincingly. They can reproduce the reasoning, discuss the relevant concepts, and respond to follow-up questions. Perhaps they’ve memorized an extraordinarily comprehensive collection of explanations.

At what point would you become convinced that they actually understand the subject? And what evidence would justify that conclusion?

This is more than an academic thought experiment. It’s one of the central problems confronting anyone who works with artificial intelligence. Modern AI systems can answer difficult questions, explain complicated concepts, write functional software, and solve problems they’ve never encountered in precisely the same form. Their capabilities are impressive, and increasingly useful.

But there’s a distinction we frequently overlook:

Producing a correct answer, having good reasons to believe that answer, and understanding why the answer is correct are three different things.

Understanding that distinction changes how we evaluate AI, how we decide whether to trust it, and how we design systems around it. To understand why, we need to visit a branch of philosophy that’s been asking remarkably similar questions for thousands of years.

01 Being right versus knowing why

The philosophy of knowing things

Epistemology is the philosophical study of knowledge. The word comes from the Greek epistēmē, meaning knowledge, and logos, commonly associated with study or reason.

At its heart, epistemology investigates a deceptively difficult question: What makes something knowledge rather than merely a belief that happens to be true?

Consider a simple statement: “The Earth revolves around the Sun.” You probably believe this. You’re also correct, at least in the ordinary astronomical sense. But why do you believe it?

Perhaps a teacher told you. Perhaps you’ve studied astronomy and understand the observations supporting heliocentrism. Perhaps you’ve never examined the evidence personally, but trust the scientific institutions responsible for establishing it.

These are different routes to the same conclusion. Does accepting reliable testimony count as knowledge? Must you personally understand the evidence? Can you know something without understanding the mechanisms that make it true? Philosophers have been wrestling with these distinctions since antiquity.

Plato and the problem of justified belief

In the fourth century BCE, Plato explored the nature of knowledge in Theaetetus. Among the possibilities examined is the idea that knowledge involves true judgment accompanied by an account or explanation. The dialogue doesn’t settle on a definitive answer, but helped establish questions that would shape centuries of inquiry.

A familiar starting point in modern discussions is the justified true belief account. Its status as a historically accepted definition is disputed. Under this framework, knowledge requires three conditions:

01 Belief
You accept a proposition as true.
02 Truth
The proposition actually is true.
03 Justification
You have adequate reasons for accepting it.

This seems intuitive. If I believe it’s raining, it really is raining, and I can see the rain falling outside my window, then I appear to know that it’s raining.

But what happens when all three conditions are satisfied and the conclusion is still correct only because of luck?

The stopped clock problem: Being right isn’t enough

Imagine you’re walking through a train station. You glance at the large analog clock above the entrance. Its hands show three o’clock. You conclude that it’s 3:00 PM. And you’re right.

Unfortunately, the clock stopped working exactly twelve hours earlier.

You have a true belief and what ordinarily seems like a reasonable justification: you checked a public clock. Yet something feels wrong about claiming that you knew the time. You were correct by coincidence.

This illustrates a problem famously explored by Edmund Gettier in his 1963 paper, Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?. Gettier presented cases in which people held justified, true beliefs that nevertheless seemed insufficient to count as knowledge.

These cases expose a problem of epistemic luck: your conclusion can be correct even when the process that produced it doesn’t reliably connect you to the truth. The stopped clock illustrates the broader Gettier problem; it is not one of Gettier’s original cases.

The AI equivalent of a stopped clock

Imagine an AI assistant responsible for answering questions about a company’s brand standards. Someone asks, “What’s our approved primary blue?” The assistant returns the correct hexadecimal value.

Success, right? Not necessarily. Suppose the assistant retrieved that value from a brand guide published four years ago. The guide is obsolete, but the primary blue happens to be one of the few specifications that hasn’t changed.

The answer is correct. The source is outdated. And the system has given us no reason to believe it will remain reliable when asked about something that has changed.

A correct result is evidence of success on a particular task. It isn’t necessarily evidence of a reliable method.

02 Performance versus understanding

Can something manipulate information without understanding it?

Consider this sequence of characters: #1D5080. By itself, it might not mean much. If you recognize hexadecimal color notation, you can interpret it as a particular shade of blue. Within an organization’s design system, it might represent an approved brand color. And if you understand the organization’s standards, you might know when to use it, which combinations are permitted, and when exceptions apply.

One sequence of characters. Several layers of interpretation.

There’s a useful distinction here between syntax and semantics. Syntax concerns the structure and arrangement of symbols. Semantics concerns their meaning. The hexadecimal value has a defined structure; interpreting it as a color depends on a notation system, and interpreting it as a brand standard depends on organizational context.

A system can process the same characters at multiple levels without necessarily possessing every kind of understanding we associate with those interpretations. A calculator offers a simple analogy. It can multiply two numbers and return the correct result, but we don’t ordinarily claim that it understands multiplication in the same way a mathematician does.

Modern language models are substantially more sophisticated. They learn complex relationships between concepts, generate explanations, solve novel problems, and generalize beyond specific training examples. So where should we draw the line?

The Chinese Room: Does convincing behavior prove understanding?

In 1980, John Searle published Minds, Brains, and Programs (PDF), introducing what became known as the Chinese Room argument.

Imagine a person locked inside a room. They speak English but don’t understand Chinese. People outside pass written Chinese questions through a slot. Inside is an enormous collection of instructions explaining how to manipulate the incoming symbols and construct appropriate responses.

The person follows the instructions meticulously. They don’t understand the characters; they recognize patterns, consult the rules, and return the prescribed combinations of symbols. Yet the responses are so convincing that people outside believe they’re communicating with someone fluent in Chinese.

Does the person inside the room understand Chinese?

Searle’s answer was no. He argued that manipulating symbols according to formal rules isn’t, by itself, sufficient to produce genuine understanding. Successfully processing the syntax of language was different from grasping its semantics.

What if the room understands, even if the person doesn’t?

One important objection, the systems reply, challenges the assumption that we’re examining the correct entity. Perhaps the person doesn’t understand Chinese. But the person isn’t the entire system.

The complete system includes the instructions, stored information, intermediate processing, and procedures that collectively produce meaningful responses. Why assume that understanding must reside in the individual following the instructions rather than in the system as a whole? After all, we don’t ordinarily attribute a human being’s full understanding to any individual neuron.

Searle rejected this objection, but the debate remains significant. Other responses question whether understanding requires interaction with the physical world, whether appropriate computational organization might be sufficient, and whether behavioral evidence can justify attributing understanding to machines.

The Chinese Room is a philosophical argument, not an experimental demonstration that machines cannot understand language. Its lasting value lies in the question it forces us to confront: When a system behaves as though it understands something, what would justify treating that behavior as genuine understanding? The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s overview explores the argument and its replies in detail.

There’s no universally accepted answer. But we can investigate what AI systems are capable of doing without first settling every philosophical question about what those capabilities mean.

Modern AI complicates the old arguments

You’ll sometimes hear people dismiss large language models by saying they “just predict the next token.” This describes an important part of how many language models are trained and generate text. It doesn’t settle what capabilities can emerge from that process.

A training objective describes what a system is optimized to do. It doesn’t automatically establish the limits of what the resulting system can learn. Language models develop internal representations that encode relationships between words, concepts, structures, and patterns in their training data. These can support capabilities considerably more sophisticated than simple memorization: translating, solving mathematical problems, writing software, recognizing analogies, and applying familiar concepts in unfamiliar contexts.

At the same time, demonstrating these capabilities doesn’t establish that a model understands language in precisely the way humans do. We should resist two tempting conclusions:

  • “AI predicts tokens, therefore it cannot reason.”
  • “AI can reason, therefore it must understand exactly as humans do.”

Neither follows automatically. There are empirical questions about what models can represent, how they generalize, and where their reasoning fails. There are also philosophical questions about whether those capabilities amount to genuine understanding. These questions overlap, but aren’t interchangeable.

Philosophical uncertainty shouldn’t become an excuse for engineering paralysis. We don’t need to solve the nature of machine understanding before deciding whether an assistant can reliably answer questions about a design system. We do need a better way to evaluate its answers.

03 What justifies trust

From philosophical knowledge to operational trust

Let’s change the question. Instead of asking, “Does this AI genuinely know what it’s talking about?” ask:

What would justify trusting this particular answer?

We can investigate that question by checking the claim and its evidence. A useful starting framework is Claim → Evidence → Confidence.

1. Claim: What is the system actually asserting?

Suppose our assistant now retrieves the current standard, Inter, but responds, “The approved font for all company presentations is Inter.” Before accepting that broader claim, we need to understand its scope. Does “all company presentations” include internal presentations? External sales materials? Campaign-specific decks? Historical templates?

An answer can be factually correct in one context and misleading in another. Sometimes the first problem is whether the question has been defined precisely enough for a single right answer to exist.

2. Evidence: What supports the claim?

Now suppose the assistant cites a brand guide. That’s useful, but insufficient on its own. Does the document actually support the claim? Is it authoritative, current, and applicable to this use case? Has another document superseded it?

This is where provenance becomes important: the origin and history of information. For an AI system, it might include which document supplied a claim, which version was used, when it was published, and whether the information was transformed or interpreted along the way.

Provenance isn’t synonymous with truth. An authoritative document can be outdated. A current document can contain errors. A valid instruction can be applied in the wrong context. Provenance helps us evaluate evidence; it doesn’t eliminate the need to interpret it correctly.

3. Confidence: How strongly should we accept the claim?

“Inter is the required font for all presentations.” The sentence is clear, decisive, and authoritative. None of those qualities establishes its correctness.

In statistics, calibration concerns how closely predicted probabilities correspond to observed outcomes. If a forecasting system assigns an 80% probability to a large collection of events, approximately 80% of those events should occur if the forecasts are well calibrated.

A model saying “I’m 95% certain” hasn’t necessarily produced a statistically calibrated probability (that percentage sign is doing a lot of heavy lifting). And telling a model to express uncertainty more frequently doesn’t automatically improve its ability to distinguish correct claims from incorrect ones.

Confidence should be justified by evidence, not inferred from how confidently an answer is written.

Research note: confidence and calibration

Band and colleagues’ research on linguistic calibration examines how generated language can help users make calibrated probabilistic judgments. MetaFaith investigates a related but distinct question: whether a model’s expressed uncertainty faithfully reflects its intrinsic uncertainty.

Faithfully expressing a model’s uncertainty and accurately predicting whether its claims are true are different goals. A faithful report of confidence can still be poorly calibrated to reality.

When explanations and citations create false confidence

Now ask two assistants which font to use for a standard company presentation. The first says, “Use Inter.” The second says, “Use Inter, according to the 2026 Campaign Brief.” Both match the current brand standard in our example.

The second response appears more trustworthy because it offers a source. But suppose the cited campaign brief contains no typography instructions. The answer is correct, yet its citation does not support it. A reader who trusts that citation may carry the same confidence into the assistant’s next answer.

A similar issue arises when language models explain their own reasoning. An explanation can sound logical without faithfully representing the factors responsible for the output. Research such as The Probabilities Also Matter investigates how to assess this faithfulness.

This doesn’t make explanations useless. They’re valuable for communicating ideas, identifying assumptions, and exposing reasoning we can independently check.

An explanation isn’t automatically an audit trail. A generated citation isn’t automatically proof. A confident answer isn’t automatically a well-supported conclusion.

These are different forms of evidence, each with limitations. The distinction becomes especially important when we build systems for repeated organizational use.

The problem might not be the model

Imagine your organization has built an assistant to answer questions about internal standards. It’s useful, but employees notice inconsistencies. Sometimes it gives outdated guidance, invents rules, or provides different answers to apparently identical questions.

Eventually, someone suggests: “We need a smarter model.” Perhaps they’re right. But what evidence supports that diagnosis?

An assistant isn’t just a language model. Depending on its architecture, it may include system instructions, reference documents, retrieval mechanisms, tools, context-selection logic, and workflows governing how information is processed. Failures can emerge from any of these components or their interactions.

Consider an assistant with three documents:

  • A company-wide brand guide from 2024.
  • A campaign-specific style guide from 2025.
  • An internal presentation template from 2026.

Each contains different typography instructions. Which should it follow? The newest document? Not necessarily. The company-wide guide? Perhaps, but a campaign-specific exception might override it. The most relevant document? Only if the system can identify the task and determine which guidance applies.

This may be an information-governance and systems-design problem. A more capable model won’t automatically resolve it.

Diagnose the failure before prescribing the solution

The underlying information may be unreliable. Relevant documents may exist without clear rules for precedence. Retrieval may fail to include the correct source. The model may receive the right information but misinterpret it or overlook an exception. And sometimes the model itself is the constraint: a task requires capabilities it cannot perform reliably, even with complete, unambiguous information.

These explanations aren’t mutually exclusive. A poorly organized knowledge base can interact with inconsistent retrieval and a tendency to resolve ambiguity through plausible-sounding guesses. The causes may be distributed across the system.

Replacing the model should be a hypothesis to test, not an automatic first response.

What a defensible investigation looks like

Start by collecting failures and a representative sample of the questions the assistant is expected to answer. A few memorable mistakes alone won’t tell you how it performs across its intended use cases.

For each example, establish what a successful answer would look like and which evidence should support it. Then examine what happened:

  • Did the system have access to the correct source?
  • Was that source included in the context used to generate the answer?
  • Did the assistant select the wrong document or misinterpret the right one?
  • Was the question ambiguous?
  • Did the answer change when the same inputs were tested again?

These observations help separate symptoms from possible causes. Once you identify a plausible cause, make a targeted change and test again.

Perhaps the solution is explicit document precedence, excluding obsolete material, or improving retrieval. Perhaps it’s narrowing which tasks the assistant can answer without human review. Or perhaps controlled testing demonstrates that a stronger model performs meaningfully better with the same correct information.

The recommendation should follow from evidence. This is epistemology translated into engineering practice: asking whether our beliefs about the system’s reliability are justified.

What David Hume would say about your evaluation results

Suppose you test an assistant against 500 questions. It answers 490 correctly. You can reasonably conclude that it performs well on that set. How much confidence should that give you about the next 500 questions?

In the eighteenth century, David Hume explored the problem of induction: why should observations of the past justify expectations about the future?

We’ve observed the Sun rising every morning and expect it to rise tomorrow. That expectation is extraordinarily reasonable. But Hume argued that we cannot establish the uniformity of nature through deductive reasoning alone; justifying induction by appealing to its past success risks circularity. The Stanford overview of the problem of induction examines this argument and responses to it.

Machine learning faces a related practical difficulty. Success on historical examples doesn’t guarantee success under new conditions. Statistical learning theory gives us mathematical tools for analyzing generalization under particular assumptions. It doesn’t make those assumptions disappear.

An evaluation set can represent one environment while missing important situations in another. An assistant might perform beautifully on routine brand questions yet struggle with ambiguous exceptions, unusual document combinations, or newly introduced standards.

So evaluation should ask more than “How often was the system correct?” It should also ask, “How representative were the tests, and under what conditions should we expect the results to generalize?” That’s a reason to evaluate continuously, rather than treating one successful benchmark as permanent proof of reliability.

What if you only have a week to fix it?

In an ideal world, every organization would have perfectly maintained documentation, rigorous evaluation, and ample time to investigate every failure. In reality, leadership often wants a solution by end of day.

That makes epistemological thinking more important. Good judgment means understanding which uncertainties matter, what evidence is available, and how much risk is acceptable.

If an assistant is giving unreliable brand guidance and you have one week, you probably shouldn’t begin by rebuilding the entire knowledge architecture. A proportionate approach might be to narrow its supported use cases, identify a small collection of authoritative references, resolve the most consequential conflicts, and test the revised configuration against known failures and representative questions. For questions that remain ambiguous or consequential, require human verification.

This produces a system with more clearly defined boundaries and better-understood limitations. Those boundaries are part of what makes it trustworthy. An assistant that reliably answers a narrower set of questions can be more useful than one that confidently attempts everything.

The objective is to establish where reliance on the AI’s outputs is justified.

Trustworthy AI is an evidence problem

We began with two students who received perfect exam scores. One understood the subject; the other memorized the answers. We asked whether identical performance demonstrated identical knowledge.

We can investigate an assistant’s reliability without settling every question about its understanding. When someone asks whether it can be trusted, we can make the question specific:

  • What is the system claiming?
  • What evidence supports that claim?
  • Where did the information come from?
  • Under what circumstances is it reliable?
  • What happens when information is incomplete, contradictory, or outdated?
  • What observations would cause us to revise our confidence?

Those questions won’t resolve every philosophical debate about artificial intelligence. But they’ll help us build better systems—and make better decisions about when to trust them.

The most useful question isn’t always whether an AI knows something. Sometimes it’s whether we have good reasons to believe what it’s telling us.
